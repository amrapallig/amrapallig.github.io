Amrapalli Garanaik, Ph.D.

Assistant Professor (Senior Research) · Physical Oceanographer · Fluid Dynamicist
College of Earth, Ocean, and Atmospheric Sciences · Oregon State University

I study the fluid mechanics of turbulent mixing, transport, and multiscale interactions in geophysical and environmental flows. My work combines theory, high-resolution simulations (DNS and LES), Earth system modeling, observations, and GPU-accelerated computing with physics-informed machine learning. The aim is to understand processes too small for climate models to resolve and to represent them better in large-scale Earth system models.

What I work on

Research

My work bridges scales, from centimeter-scale turbulence in the lab and in DNS to global ocean models, with the goal of building physically grounded mixing parameterizations for climate prediction.

Current projects

OSBL turbulence

Nonlocal second-moment upper-ocean boundary layer modeling

Physically based, nonlocal second-moment turbulence closures built from high-resolution simulations and theory, to improve mixing in the ocean surface boundary layer.

Earth system modeling

Plume-based turbulence closures for Earth system models

Nonlocal, high-order and mass-flux parameterizations of the ocean surface boundary layer, implemented and tested in E3SM/MPAS-Ocean.

Air–sea interaction

Air–sea interaction from Saildrone observations

How sea-surface temperature variability and surface currents shape air–sea momentum and heat fluxes.

Upper-ocean dynamics

Diurnal warm layers and stabilizing surface fluxes

Simulations and observations of how diurnal heating, variable winds and surface waves affect turbulence in the upper ocean.

Coupled modeling

Coupling of the tropical air–sea boundary layers

Observational analysis and multiscale coupled modeling of ocean–atmosphere boundary-layer processes.

HPC · Regional modeling

Wildfire and regional coupled modeling

Wildfire and regional coupled modeling on Oregon State University's Huang Complex supercomputer.

Research highlights

Earth system modeling

A new boundary layer scheme for Earth system models

We developed a physically motivated hybrid mass-flux/high-order mixing scheme for ocean surface boundary layer (OSBL) turbulence and implemented it in MPAS-Ocean, the ocean component of the DOE's Energy Exascale Earth System Model (E3SM). It captures OSBL physics driven by buoyancy, wind and waves efficiently, which makes it a promising way to reduce climate-model biases.

JAMES 2024 →
DNS · Theory

Stratified turbulence through direct numerical simulation

DNS of stably stratified turbulence reveals flow dynamics and mixing. It provides physics-based parameterizations of diapycnal mixing and ways to infer them from quantities that can be measured in the field.

JFM 2019 Phys. Fluids 2022 Phys. Fluids 2018
Observations

Air–sea interaction from satellite and in-situ data

As a member of the air–sea interaction and velocity working groups in NASA's Sub-Mesoscale Ocean Dynamics Experiment (S-MODE), I study surface flux estimates and their response to SST and ocean currents. At sea, I deployed uCTDs aboard R/V Roger Revelle in the Bay of Bengal during the 2014 ASIRI program.

Oceanography 2016 →
HPC · LES

Large eddy simulations of the ocean on GPUs

We run ocean LES with Oceananigans.jl on NVIDIA A100, V100 and H100 GPUs and on Grace Hopper systems at Oregon State University, which makes high-resolution studies of Langmuir turbulence and sea-ice-lead mixing possible.

OSM 2024 abstract →
Mesoscale · Lagrangian

Mesoscale mixing from Lagrangian particles

Using the LIGHT particle-tracking package (Lagrangian, In situ, Global, High-performance particle Tracking) in eddy-resolving MPAS-Ocean (E3SM) on NERSC supercomputers, we quantify horizontal and vertical variability of isopycnal diffusivity to develop better parameterizations.

Manuscript in preparation →
Laboratory

Experimental fluid mechanics

We measured turbulent open-channel flows with Acoustic Doppler Velocimetry (ADV) and Laser Doppler Anemometry (LDA) on a moving traverse in a recirculating flume at Colorado State University, and the results show how the two sensors compare in practice.
